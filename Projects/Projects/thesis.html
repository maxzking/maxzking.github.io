Modeling Stylus Dynamics with High-Speed Photography Undergraduate Honor's Thesis Advisor: Marjorie Shaprio

Abstract

The research for this thesis was conducted from Fall 2022 to Spring 2024 at Project IRENE’s office in Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. This project was conceived as an effort to understand how styli move on cylindrical record surfaces in order to improve audio created from optical reconstruction by IRENE. Three (3) approaches were devised in this aim: recreation of audio using high-speed photography, measurement of cylinder cutter angle, and piezoelectric actuator systems.

This article focuses on the high-speed photography approach of audio reproduciton. For more information, you can download my thesis here.

Background

Many early audio recordings were produced in low quantity and are archived in private or library collections. Over the years, they lose their integrity from mold, cracks, and scratches. Project IRENE develops optical reconstruction methods to recover the audio from these recordings without further damaging them.

Figure 1: The Right of the People to Rule / Teddy Roosevelt. Edison Blue Amberol: 3707. 1919, Courtesy of UCSB Cylinder Audio Archive

Project IRENE is a laboratory located at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory that digitizes audio from early or rare mechanical audio recordings via high-resolution 3D imaging. IRENE uses a confocal imaging probe to scan the depth of the grooves along the recording's surface. Using in-house image processing software, IRENE tracks how the depth of the groove modulates over time, and from this recreates the audio of the recording. For my thesis, I investigated how a stylus moves over the grooves of a recording during traditional mechanical playback to improve IRENE's understanding of the relationship between groove shape and audio.

Recreating Audio with High-Speed Photography

The Chronos 1.4 is a high-speed camera capable of capturing the motion of a stylus playing a mechanical recording at a rate of 10,000 fps. During an experiment, the stylus motion is filmed by the Chronos 1.4 and the mechanically transduced audio is recorded by a Focusrite 2i2.

Figure 3: In-Lab Experimental Setup

After playback has finished, the video is sent to National Instruments Vision Assistant software to track the frame-by-frame position of the stylus. It is known that the audio transduced by the stylus cartridge is proportional to the stylus's velocity. Therefore, audio can be recreated from the video by measuring the velocity of the stylus. We achieve this by taking the first order discrete derivative of the stylus position.

Figure 4: Image of a Stylus Tip and Cantilever Captured by the Chronos 1.4

At this point, there are two audio files: one from the Chronos 1.4, and one from the Focusrite 2i2. The audio files are systematically synchronized by lagging one audio file one sample at a time and finding the maximum correlation coefficient (via the Pearson method). Correlation values between two variables span from -1 and 1, where values near -1 indicate a strong inverse relationship, values near 0 indicate minimal association, and values near 1 indicate a strong direct relationship. In the example below, the maximum correlation for a cylindrical recording titled "Stars and Stripes Forever" is 0.69, suggesting that there is a strong relationship between audio created via high-speed photography and mechanically transduced audio.

Conclusion

Audio reproduced via high-speed photography of stylus motion has high correlation to mechanically transduced audio, suggesting it is an apt method of recovering information from stylus vibrations. The high-speed photography method provides an independent method of measuring qualities of stylus motion, which can be used to improve the understanding of the relationship between groove shape and the resultant audio. This can be of great benefit to IRENE and the preservation of historic mechanical audio recordings.
